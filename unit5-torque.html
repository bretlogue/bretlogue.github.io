The Big Picture: Linear ↔ Rotational

Every concept in this unit is the rotational twin of something from Units 1–4. The table below is the most powerful study tool in this unit — learn the left column, then the right column maps directly onto it.

Exam strategy
When you can't remember a rotational formula, ask: "What is the linear version?" Then swap m→I, v→ω, a→α, F→τ. You'll get the correct rotational equation almost every time.
5.1

Rotational Kinematics

Rotating objects are described with angular quantities that mirror linear kinematics exactly. Angles must be in radians (not degrees) because the v = rω link only holds in radians.

Angular velocity: ω = Δθ / Δt  (rad/s)
Angular acceleration: α = Δω / Δt  (rad/s²)
Linear-angular links: v = rω    atangential = rα

The four kinematic equations from Unit 1 translate directly — swap x→θ, v→ω, a→α:

Linear equationRotational equivalent
v = v₀ + atω = ω₀ + αt
Δx = v₀t + ½at²Δθ = ω₀t + ½αt²
v² = v₀² + 2aΔxω² = ω₀² + 2αΔθ
Δx = ½(v₀+v)tΔθ = ½(ω₀+ω)t
Radians vs. degrees
Always convert degrees to radians before plugging into kinematic equations: θrad = θdeg × π/180. And 1 rpm = 2π/60 rad/s.
1
Worked Example — Spinning Up a Wheel

A wheel starts from rest and reaches 120 rpm in 4 s with constant angular acceleration. Find α and the number of revolutions completed.

Given: ω₀ = 0, ωf = 120 rpm = 4π rad/s, t = 4 s
Step 1 — convert rpm to rad/s
ωf = 120 × (2π/60) = 4π ≈ 12.57 rad/s
Step 2 — find α
α = (ωf − ω₀) / t = 4π / 4 = π ≈ 3.14 rad/s²
Step 3 — find Δθ and convert to revolutions
Δθ = ½αt² = ½(π)(16) = 8π rad = 4 revolutions
α ≈ 3.14 rad/s²  |  4 revolutions

5.2

Torque

Torque is the rotational equivalent of force — it causes angular acceleration. A force only produces torque if it has a component perpendicular to the line from the pivot to the point of application. Force aimed directly at the pivot produces zero torque.

τ = r · F · sin(θ)    [N·m]
Equivalently: τ = r⊥ · F    (r⊥ = lever arm)
CCW positive, CW negative (standard convention)
Two ways to read the same formula
τ = rF sinθ can be parsed either way:
(1) Full distance r × perpendicular component of force: F⊥ = F sinθ
(2) Full force F × perpendicular distance to line of action: r⊥ = r sinθ
Pick whichever interpretation matches the geometry of the problem.
2
Worked Example — Force at an Angle on a Beam

A 0.9 m door is pushed with 15 N at 50° from the door surface (= 40° from perpendicular). Find the torque about the hinge.

Given: r = 0.9 m, F = 15 N, θ (between F and r) = 90° − 50° = 40°... use the angle between r and F directly
Identify θ between force vector and position vector r
If the force is 50° from the door surface, and r lies along the door surface, then the angle between F and r is 50°:
τ = rF sin(50°) = (0.9)(15)(0.766) = 10.3 N·m
τ = 10.3 N·m (CCW)

Compare: if force were perpendicular to door (sin 90° = 1), τ = 13.5 N·m — maximum possible. Any angle away from 90° reduces the torque.

5.3

Rotational Inertia

Rotational inertia (I) is the rotational equivalent of mass — it measures resistance to angular acceleration. It depends not just on how much mass an object has, but on where that mass sits relative to the rotation axis. Mass farther from the axis contributes much more to I (by r²).

Point mass: I = mr²
System of point masses: Itotal = Σmr²
(AP exam provides extended-body formulas — you do not memorize them)
Solid disk / cylinder
I = ½MR²
Average mass distance < R; less rotational inertia than hoop
Hoop / thin ring
I = MR²
All mass at R; maximum I for given M and R
Solid sphere
I = ⅖MR²
Mass near center; smallest I of common shapes
Thin rod (about center)
I = &frac{1}{12}ML²
About end: I = ⅓ML² (farther axis → larger I)
Hoop vs. disk racing down a ramp — the AP classic
A hoop and a solid disk of equal M and R start from rest at the top of an incline. The disk wins because it has lower rotational inertia (½MR² vs. MR²). Less of the gravitational PE is "locked up" as rotational KE, leaving more for translational KE — faster linear speed at the bottom. This is the most commonly tested application of rotational inertia.
Parallel axis theorem
Shifting the rotation axis away from the center of mass always increases I: I = Icm + Md², where d is the distance between the two parallel axes. A rod rotating about its end has I = ⅓ML², which is 4× larger than &frac{1}{12}ML² about the center.
3
Worked Example — Two-Mass System

Two 2 kg masses are attached to opposite ends of a 0.8 m massless rod pivoted at its center. Find I.

Given: m = 2 kg each, r = 0.4 m each (half of 0.8 m)
Apply I = Σmr²
I = (2)(0.4)² + (2)(0.4)² = 0.32 + 0.32
I = 0.64 kg·m²

If the pivot moved to one end: rfar = 0.8 m, so I = (2)(0)² + (2)(0.8)² = 1.28 kg·m² — doubled because mass is now farther from the axis on average.

5.4

Newton's Second Law for Rotation: τ = Iα

A net torque causes angular acceleration, just as a net force causes linear acceleration. Rotational inertia I resists the change — larger I means the same torque produces smaller α.

τnet = Iα
Combined (rolling): ΣF = ma (translation) + Στ = Iα (rotation) + a = rα (no-slip link)
Strategy for combined translational + rotational problems
For objects that roll or have a string-wrapped pulley:
  1. Draw the FBD identifying all forces
  2. Write ΣF = ma for the translational direction
  3. Write Στ = Iα about the rotation axis
  4. Link with a = rα (rolling without slipping)
  5. Solve the resulting system of equations
4
Worked Example — Mass Hanging from a Pulley

A 5 kg mass hangs from a cord wrapped around a solid disk pulley (M = 4 kg, R = 0.3 m) free to rotate. Find the acceleration of the hanging mass.

Given: m = 5 kg, M = 4 kg, R = 0.3 m, Idisk = ½MR²
Step 1 — translation (hanging mass)
mg − T = ma  →  T = m(g−a)   (i)
Step 2 — rotation (pulley): τ = T·R = Iα
TR = (½MR²)(a/R) = ½MRa  →  T = ½Ma   (ii)
Step 3 — substitute (ii) into (i)
m(g−a) = ½Ma  →  mg = a(m + ½M) = a(5+2)
a = (5)(9.8)/7
a = 7.0 m/s² (vs. 9.8 without pulley mass — rotational inertia slows the system)
5.5

Rotational Equilibrium: Στ = 0

When the net torque on an object is zero, it rotates at constant angular velocity (including ω = 0). Full static equilibrium requires both ΣF = 0 (no translational acceleration) and Στ = 0 (no angular acceleration).

Rotational equilibrium: Στ = 0   →   ΣτCCW = ΣτCW
Pivot selection — the most important FRQ technique in this unit
You can choose any point as your pivot. Strategically choose the point where an unknown force acts — that force has r = 0 and vanishes from the torque equation entirely. This eliminates one unknown without any extra work.
5
Worked Example — Non-Uniform Beam

A 4 m uniform beam (M = 10 kg) is supported at 1.5 m from the left end. A 20 kg block hangs from the left end. What downward force at the right end keeps the beam horizontal?

Given: L = 4 m, M = 10 kg, mblock = 20 kg, pivot at x = 1.5 m from left
Step 1 — place pivot at support (eliminates unknown reaction force)
Distances from pivot: block (left end) = 1.5 m CW; beam CM (x=2 m) = 0.5 m CCW; applied force (x=4 m) = 2.5 m
Step 2 — set Στ = 0
+(10)(9.8)(0.5) + F(2.5) − (20)(9.8)(1.5) = 0
49 + 2.5F = 294  →  F = 245/2.5
F = 98 N downward at the right end

Unit 5 takeaways

  • Every linear quantity has a rotational twin. Swap m→I, v→ω, a→α, F→τ to convert any linear law to its rotational equivalent.
  • τ = rF sinθ. Maximum torque at θ = 90°; zero torque when force points directly at (or away from) the pivot.
  • I = Σmr² for point masses. The r² dependence means mass twice as far contributes four times more. A hoop has more I than a disk of the same mass and radius; the disk wins the ramp race.
  • τnet = Iα is Newton's second law for rotation. Link to translation via a = rα (rolling without slipping).
  • Rotational equilibrium: Στ = 0. Choose the pivot strategically — place it where an unknown force acts to eliminate it from the equation immediately.
  • Units: torque in N·m (rotational tendency, vector); energy/work in J = N·m (scalar). Same unit, different physical meaning.
