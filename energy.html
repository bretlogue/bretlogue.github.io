3.1

Translational Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because of its motion. It depends on mass and the square of speed, which means doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy — a relationship the AP exam tests constantly with proportional reasoning questions.

KE = ½mv²
Key insight
Because v is squared, kinetic energy grows much faster than speed. A car going 60 mph has four times the kinetic energy of the same car at 30 mph, not twice. This is part of why stopping distances increase so dramatically at higher speeds — the brakes must remove four times the energy.

Kinetic energy is a scalar (no direction) and depends on the reference frame of the observer — a passenger sitting still on a moving train has zero KE relative to the train, but nonzero KE relative to the ground.

3.2

Work and the Work-Energy Theorem

Work is the transfer of energy that occurs when a force acts through a displacement. Only the component of force parallel to the displacement does work — this is exactly the dot product idea from vector math.

W = F·d·cos(θ)     (θ = angle between force and displacement)
Work-Energy Theorem:   Wnet = ΔKE = ½mvf² − ½mvi²
Positive work
Force has a component in the same direction as displacement. Increases KE. (Example: gravity on a falling object.)
Negative work
Force has a component opposite to displacement. Decreases KE. (Example: friction opposing motion, gravity on a rising object.)
Common mistake
A force doing zero work doesn't mean the object isn't moving — it means the force is perpendicular to the displacement. Normal force on a person walking horizontally does zero work, even though the person is clearly moving, because normal force points straight up while displacement is horizontal: cos(90°) = 0.
Why the work-energy theorem is powerful
It lets you find a final speed directly from forces and distances — no need for time or acceleration. If you know all the forces doing work and the distance traveled, you skip kinematics entirely.
1
Worked Example — Work-Energy Theorem with Friction

A 4 kg block slides across a floor at 6 m/s and comes to rest after 3 m due to friction. Find the friction force and the coefficient of kinetic friction.

Given: m = 4 kg, vi = 6 m/s, vf = 0 m/s, d = 3 m, g = 9.8 m/s²
Find: fk and μk
Step 1 — apply the work-energy theorem
The only horizontal force doing work is friction, and it's negative (opposes motion):
Wfriction = ΔKE = ½m(vf² − vi²) = ½(4)(0² − 6²)
Wfriction = ½(4)(−36) = −72 J
Step 2 — solve for the friction force
W = F·d·cos(180°) = −F·d, so:
−72 = −fk(3) → fk = 24 N
fk = 24 N
Step 3 — find μk using N = mg
μk = fk / N = 24 / (4 × 9.8) = 24 / 39.2
μk = 0.61

3.3

Potential Energy: Gravitational & Elastic

Potential energy is energy stored due to an object's position or configuration. It only exists for conservative forces — forces where work done depends only on starting and ending position, never on the path taken.

Gravitational PE:   PEgrav = mgh
Elastic (spring) PE:   PEelastic = ½kx²    (Hooke's Law: Fspring = −kx)
Conservative forces
Gravity, spring force. Work is path-independent. Can be stored as potential energy. Wconservative = −ΔPE.
Nonconservative forces
Friction, air resistance, applied pushes. Work is path-dependent. No potential energy function exists — energy is dissipated, usually as heat.
Key insight
Gravitational PE is always relative to a chosen reference height — only changes in PE matter physically. You can set h = 0 anywhere convenient (the ground, a tabletop, the lowest point of motion) as long as you're consistent throughout the problem.
2
Worked Example — Spring Compressed by a Block

A spring with k = 200 N/m is compressed 0.15 m by a 0.5 kg block. If released on a frictionless surface, find the block's launch speed.

Given: k = 200 N/m, x = 0.15 m, m = 0.5 kg, frictionless
Find: v (launch speed)
Step 1 — find elastic PE stored in the spring
PEelastic = ½kx² = ½(200)(0.15²) = ½(200)(0.0225)
PEelastic = 2.25 J
Step 2 — all elastic PE converts to KE (frictionless, conservative)
½mv² = 2.25 → v² = 2(2.25)/0.5 = 9
v = 3.0 m/s
3.4

Conservation of Energy

When only conservative forces act, total mechanical energy (KE + PE) stays constant — energy just changes form. When nonconservative forces (like friction) act, mechanical energy is not conserved, and the work they do must be tracked explicitly.

Conservative forces only:   KEi + PEi = KEf + PEf
With nonconservative forces:   KEi + PEi + Wnc = KEf + PEf
Why this method beats forces alone
Energy conservation solves problems where the path is curved or unknown — like the speed at the bottom of an oddly-shaped ramp. You don't need to know the shape, only the start and end heights. Force methods (kinematics) would require knowing the exact path; energy methods don't care.
3
Worked Example — Roller Coaster with Friction

A 300 kg cart starts from rest at the top of a 25 m hill. Friction does −8000 J of work as it descends. Find the speed at the bottom.

Given: m = 300 kg, h = 25 m, vi = 0, Wfriction = −8000 J, g = 9.8 m/s²
Find: vf
Step 1 — set up the energy equation
KEi + PEi + Wnc = KEf + PEf
0 + mgh + Wfriction = ½mvf² + 0
Step 2 — substitute known values
(300)(9.8)(25) + (−8000) = ½(300)vf²
73500 − 8000 = 150vf² → 65500 = 150vf²
Step 3 — solve for vf
vf² = 436.7 → vf = √436.7
vf = 20.9 m/s

3.5

Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred. The same amount of work can be done quickly (high power) or slowly (low power) — power captures that distinction.

Average power:   P = ΔE / Δt = W / t
Instantaneous power:   P = F·v    (force times instantaneous velocity)
Common mistake
Power and energy are easy to confuse. Energy is measured in joules (J) and represents a total amount; power is measured in watts (W = J/s) and represents a rate. Two engines can do the same total work but have very different power if one takes longer.
4
Worked Example — Motor Lifting a Crate

A motor lifts a 150 kg crate at a constant 0.8 m/s. What power must the motor supply?

Given: m = 150 kg, v = 0.8 m/s (constant, so Fmotor = weight), g = 9.8 m/s²
Find: P
Step 1 — find the required force
Constant velocity means equilibrium, so the motor's force equals the crate's weight:
F = mg = (150)(9.8) = 1470 N
Step 2 — apply P = Fv
P = (1470)(0.8)
P = 1176 W

Unit 3 takeaways

  • KE = ½mv² — doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy.
  • Work = F·d·cos(θ); only the force component parallel to displacement does work. Perpendicular forces do zero work.
  • The work-energy theorem (Wnet = ΔKE) lets you skip kinematics entirely when you know forces and distances.
  • Conservative forces (gravity, springs) store potential energy; nonconservative forces (friction) dissipate it.
  • Conservation of energy handles curved or unknown paths that force methods can't easily solve.
  • Power is a rate (P = W/t = Fv) — don't confuse it with energy itself.
