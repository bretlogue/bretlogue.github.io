2.1

Systems, Center of Mass & Free-Body Diagrams

A force is a push or pull resulting from the interaction between two objects. Before solving any dynamics problem, you must define your system — the object or group of objects you're analyzing. Forces from outside the system (external) can change the system's motion. Forces between objects inside the system (internal) always cancel in pairs and never affect the system's center of mass.

Key insight
Choosing your system is a strategic decision, not just a formality. Treating a car + trailer as one system makes the tension between them disappear (it's internal) — useful for finding overall acceleration, but useless if the question asks for the tension itself, which requires isolating one object.

A free-body diagram (FBD) is the primary tool of this entire unit. It shows only the external forces acting on one object, drawn as arrows from a single point (the object's center of mass).

Step 1
Isolate the object. Represent it as a single dot — don't draw the incline, table, or rope it's attached to.
Step 2
Ask "what touches it?" (normal, friction, tension, spring) and "what fields act on it?" (gravity).
Step 3
Draw each force as an arrow from the dot, roughly scaled to magnitude, pointing in its true direction.
Step 4
Label every arrow (Fg, FN, FT, fk). Don't draw x/y components on the official diagram — that's a separate scratch step.
Common mistake
Students often draw a "centripetal force" or "net force" as its own arrow on a free-body diagram. Don't. Only draw real, physical forces (gravity, normal, tension, friction, applied push/pull). Centripetal force is just the name for whatever net force happens to point toward the center — it's not a new force to add to the diagram.
2.2

Newton's First Law & Equilibrium

Newton's First Law
An object's velocity stays constant unless acted on by a net external force.
Also called the law of inertia. "Constant velocity" includes zero velocity — an object at rest stays at rest. This is the definition of translational equilibrium: when the net force on a system is zero, its acceleration is zero, and velocity does not change.

Equilibrium problems are solved by setting the sum of forces in each direction equal to zero: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0. This is one of the most common AP Physics 1 problem types — an object hanging from ropes, sitting on a table, or moving at constant velocity.

1
Worked Example — Sign Hanging from Two Ropes

A 40 N sign hangs in equilibrium from two ropes, each making a 50° angle with the ceiling. Find the tension in each rope.

Given: weight = 40 N, θ = 50° from ceiling (so 40° from horizontal... use angle as given from horizontal for vertical component), symmetric setup so T₁ = T₂ = T
Find: T
Step 1 — set up vertical equilibrium
Each rope's vertical component must support half the weight. With θ measured from the ceiling (horizontal), the vertical component of tension is T·sin(50°).
2·T·sin(50°) = 40 N
Step 2 — solve for T
T = 40 / (2·sin(50°)) = 40 / (2 × 0.766) = 40 / 1.532
T = 26.1 N in each rope

Notice the horizontal components of the two tensions point in opposite directions and cancel automatically by symmetry — that's why only the vertical equation was needed here.

2.3

Newton's Second Law

Newton's Second Law
ΣF = ma
The acceleration of a system's center of mass is directly proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass, pointing in the same direction as the net force. This is a vector equation — apply it separately in each direction: ΣFx = max and ΣFy = may.

Nearly every problem in this unit, from friction to springs to circular motion, is just a different force to plug into this one equation. The standard procedure: draw the FBD, choose a coordinate system, write ΣF = ma along each axis, then solve algebraically.

2
Worked Example — Two Blocks Connected by a String

A 3 kg block (A) on a frictionless table is connected by a string over a pulley to a hanging 2 kg block (B). Find the acceleration of the system and the tension in the string.

Given: mA = 3 kg (horizontal, frictionless), mB = 2 kg (hanging), g = 9.8 m/s²
Find: a, T
Step 1 — write Newton's second law for each block
Block A (horizontal): only tension acts horizontally → T = mAa
Block B (vertical): gravity pulls down, tension pulls up → mBg − T = mBa
Step 2 — add the two equations to eliminate T
mBg = (mA + mB)a → a = mBg / (mA + mB)
a = (2)(9.8) / (3 + 2) = 19.6 / 5
a = 3.92 m/s²
Step 3 — substitute back to find tension
T = mAa = (3)(3.92)
T = 11.76 N

This "add the equations" trick is standard for connected systems: it eliminates the internal force (tension) immediately, since the system as a whole only feels the external force mBg.

2.4

Newton's Third Law

Newton's Third Law
If object A exerts a force on object B, then B exerts an equal and opposite force on A.
These two forces act on different objects at the same time, have equal magnitude, and point in opposite directions. They never cancel each other in a single object's free-body diagram, because each force acts on a different object.
Common mistake
The normal force on a book and the book's weight are not a third-law pair, even though they're equal and opposite — they act on the same object and just happen to balance (that's Newton's first law / equilibrium, a coincidence of magnitude, not a law). The true third-law partner of "table pushes up on book" is "book pushes down on table."
How to find a third-law pair
Identify the verb and swap the subject and object. "Table pushes book up" → partner is "book pushes table down." "Earth pulls rocket down" → partner is "rocket pulls Earth up." The two forces are always the same type (both normal forces, or both gravitational forces) and act on two different bodies.
2.5

Friction

Friction is a contact force that opposes relative motion (or attempted relative motion) between two surfaces. It depends only on the normal force and the materials in contact — never on contact area or speed (in the AP Physics 1 model).

TypeEquationWhen it applies
Static frictionfs ≤ μsNObject is not sliding. Friction equals whatever is needed to prevent motion, up to a maximum.
Kinetic frictionfk = μkNObject is actively sliding. Friction is constant, opposing the sliding direction.
Common mistake
Writing fs = μsN for an object sitting calmly is wrong unless it's on the verge of slipping. Static friction is an inequality — it adjusts to match the applied force exactly, up to its maximum value. If you push a 10 N box with 4 N and it doesn't move, static friction is exactly 4 N, not μsN.
μs > μk always (static friction's maximum exceeds kinetic friction)
On a horizontal surface: N = mg
On an incline: N = mg·cos(θ)
3
Worked Example — Box Pushed Across the Floor

A 12 kg box is pushed with a horizontal force of 50 N across a floor with μk = 0.3. Find the box's acceleration.

Given: m = 12 kg, Fapplied = 50 N, μk = 0.3, g = 9.8 m/s²
Find: a
Step 1 — find the normal force
N = mg = (12)(9.8) = 117.6 N
Step 2 — find kinetic friction
fk = μkN = (0.3)(117.6) = 35.3 N
Step 3 — apply Newton's second law horizontally
a = (Fapplied − fk) / m = (50 − 35.3) / 12
a = 1.22 m/s²

2.6

Inclined Planes

On an incline, gravity must be split into two components: one parallel to the surface (causing sliding) and one perpendicular to it (balanced by the normal force). This is the most common multi-step application of everything in this unit so far.

Parallel to incline: mg·sin(θ)   (drives the object down the slope)
Perpendicular to incline: mg·cos(θ) = N   (balanced by normal force)
Acceleration down a frictionless incline: a = g·sin(θ)
Acceleration down an incline with friction: a = g(sinθ − μkcosθ)
Key insight
Before solving any incline problem with friction, compute tan(θ) and compare it to μs. If tan(θ) > μs, the object slides; if not, it stays put. This 5-second check determines your entire solution path before you write a single equation.

2.7

Uniform Circular Motion

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is still accelerating — its velocity direction constantly changes, even though its speed doesn't. This centripetal acceleration always points toward the center of the circle.

ac = v² / r     Fc = mac = mv² / r
Common mistake
"Centripetal force" is not a new, separate force — it's the name given to whichever real force (tension, friction, gravity, normal force) happens to be pointing toward the center and causing the circular motion. Never add a "centripetal force" arrow to your free-body diagram alongside the real forces; identify which existing force is providing it.

When friction provides the centripetal force (a car rounding a flat curve), the maximum speed before slipping comes from setting the maximum static friction equal to the required centripetal force:

μsmg = mv²/r  →  vmax = √(μsgr)

Notice mass cancels entirely — this is a frequent and fast multiple-choice setup on the exam.

4
Worked Example — Car Rounding a Flat Curve

A car rounds a flat curve of radius 50 m. The coefficient of static friction between tires and road is 0.6. What is the maximum speed before the car begins to skid?

Given: r = 50 m, μs = 0.6, g = 9.8 m/s²
Find: vmax
Step 1 — set max static friction equal to required centripetal force
μsmg = mv²/r  →  v = √(μsgr)
Step 2 — substitute values
v = √((0.6)(9.8)(50)) = √294
vmax = 17.1 m/s

Unit 2 takeaways

  • Define your system first — internal forces cancel, only external forces change the system's motion.
  • Draw a clean free-body diagram before writing any equation: only real forces, no components, no "net force" arrows.
  • ΣF = ma is the engine of the whole unit. Apply it separately in x and y.
  • Newton's third law pairs act on two different objects and are always the same type of force.
  • Static friction is an inequality (fs ≤ μsN); kinetic friction is fixed (fk = μkN). Check tan(θ) vs. μs first on inclines.
  • Centripetal force isn't a new force — it's whichever real force points toward the center. ac = v²/r.
