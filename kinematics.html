1.1

Scalars and Vectors in One Dimension

A scalar is a quantity with magnitude only — a number and a unit. Distance, speed, time, and mass are all scalars. A vector has both magnitude and direction — displacement, velocity, and acceleration are vectors. The distinction matters constantly in AP Physics 1, because two quantities that sound similar (distance vs. displacement, speed vs. velocity) can have very different values in the same scenario.

Distance (scalar)
Total length of the path traveled. Always positive, never decreases.
Displacement (vector)
Straight-line change in position, from start to end. Can be zero even if distance traveled is large.
Speed (scalar)
Distance ÷ time. Always positive.
Velocity (vector)
Displacement ÷ time. Can be negative, indicating direction.
Common mistake
If you walk 5 m east then 5 m west, your distance traveled is 10 m, but your displacement is 0 m. The AP exam tests this distinction constantly — always ask "are they asking for path length or net change in position?"

In one dimension, direction is represented with a sign: positive and negative. You choose which direction is positive (often "right" or "up"), and everything is measured relative to that choice. The sign of velocity tells you direction of motion; the sign of acceleration tells you which way the velocity is changing — not necessarily which way the object is moving.

Key insight
A negative acceleration does not mean "slowing down." It means acceleration points in the negative direction. If velocity and acceleration have the same sign, the object speeds up. If they have opposite signs, it slows down. Always check both signs together.
1.2

Displacement, Velocity, and Acceleration

These three quantities are connected by calculus, but AP Physics 1 only requires the algebra-based relationships: velocity is the rate of change of position, and acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. For motion with constant acceleration — the overwhelming majority of Unit 1 problems — four equations connect every combination of these variables.

EquationSolves forMissing variable
v = v₀ + atfinal velocitydisplacement (Δx)
Δx = v₀t + ½at²displacementfinal velocity (v)
v² = v₀² + 2aΔxfinal velocity (no time needed)time (t)
Δx = ½(v₀ + v)tdisplacement (average velocity form)acceleration (a)
How to choose the right equation
List your knowns and your unknown. Each equation is missing exactly one of the five kinematic variables (v₀, v, a, t, Δx). Pick the equation that's missing the variable you don't have and don't need. This single habit eliminates almost all confusion about "which formula do I use."
1
Worked Example — Braking Car

A car traveling at 24 m/s brakes with a constant acceleration of −4 m/s². How far does it travel before stopping?

Given: v₀ = 24 m/s, v = 0 m/s (stops), a = −4 m/s²
Find: Δx
Step 1 — choose the equation
We have v₀, v, and a. We want Δx and don't care about t. That points to v² = v₀² + 2aΔx.
0² = 24² + 2(−4)Δx
Step 2 — solve algebraically before plugging in numbers
0 = 576 − 8Δx → Δx = 576 / 8
Δx = 72 m

Sanity check: a positive displacement makes sense, since the car keeps moving forward as it slows. The negative acceleration matches a velocity that's decreasing toward zero — signs agree with the physical picture.

1.3

Representing Motion: Graphs

The same motion can be shown as a motion diagram, a position-time graph, a velocity-time graph, words, or equations. The AP exam constantly asks you to translate between these representations. The relationships between the three graph types are the single most tested skill in this topic.

GraphSlope meansArea under curve means
Position vs. time (x–t)velocity(not typically used)
Velocity vs. time (v–t)accelerationdisplacement
Acceleration vs. time (a–t)(not typically used)change in velocity
Key insight
The velocity-time graph is the workhorse of Unit 1. If you can read a v–t graph fluently — pulling acceleration from slope and displacement from area — you can answer the majority of kinematics questions on the exam, including ones with changing (non-constant) acceleration where the four kinematic equations don't apply.

Common mistake
Students often confuse "the v–t graph crosses zero" with "the object stops accelerating." They're unrelated. Crossing zero on a v–t graph means velocity is momentarily zero (the object is turning around); the slope — acceleration — can be nonzero the whole time.
1.4

Reference Frames and Relative Motion

All motion is described relative to a chosen reference frame. A passenger on a train moving at 20 m/s appears stationary to another passenger but is moving at 20 m/s to someone standing on the ground. Velocities add (or subtract) when switching between reference frames, as long as we're careful with signs and directions.

vA relative to C = vA relative to B + vB relative to C
2
Worked Example — Walking on a Train

A train moves at 15 m/s east relative to the ground. A passenger walks at 2 m/s east relative to the train. What is the passenger's velocity relative to the ground?

Given: vtrain/ground = +15 m/s, vpassenger/train = +2 m/s
Find: vpassenger/ground
Step 1 — add the velocities
vpassenger/ground = vpassenger/train + vtrain/ground = 2 + 15
vpassenger/ground = 17 m/s east

If the passenger instead walked toward the back of the train at 2 m/s, you'd use −2 m/s, giving 13 m/s east relative to the ground. Always assign a consistent positive direction before adding.

1.5

Vectors and Motion in Two Dimensions

Two-dimensional motion is solved by splitting it into two independent one-dimensional problems — horizontal (x) and vertical (y) — that share only one quantity: time. This is the single biggest idea in 2D kinematics. Once a vector is broken into components using sine and cosine, each direction is solved with the same four equations from Topic 1.2.

Ax = A·cos(θ)     Ay = A·sin(θ)
A = √(Ax² + Ay²)     θ = tan⁻&sup9;(Ay / Ax)

Projectile motion is the special case tested most heavily: an object launched into the air has zero acceleration horizontally (constant horizontal velocity) and constant acceleration g = 9.8 m/s² downward, vertically. The two directions never interact — gravity never affects horizontal velocity, and horizontal motion never affects how fast the object falls.

Key insight
A projectile launched at an angle takes exactly as long to rise to its peak as it takes to fall back to launch height. At the peak, vertical velocity is exactly zero — but horizontal velocity is completely unchanged from launch. Acceleration (g) never pauses, even at the peak.
3
Worked Example — Projectile Launched at an Angle

A ball is launched at 30 m/s at 40° above horizontal from level ground. Find the time of flight and the horizontal range.

Given: v₀ = 30 m/s, θ = 40°, g = 9.8 m/s², launch height = landing height
Find: time of flight (T) and range (R)
Step 1 — split into components
v₀x = 30·cos(40°) = 23.0 m/s    v₀y = 30·sin(40°) = 19.3 m/s
Step 2 — find time of flight using the vertical direction only
Since launch and landing height are equal, use Δy = 0:  0 = v₀yt − ½gt²
T = 2v₀y / g = 2(19.3) / 9.8
T = 3.94 s
Step 3 — find range using the horizontal direction (constant velocity)
R = v₀x·T = 23.0 × 3.94
R = 90.6 m

Notice that step 2 used only vertical quantities, and step 3 used only horizontal quantities — the two directions were never mixed except through the shared variable T.

Unit 1 takeaways

  • Distinguish scalars (distance, speed) from vectors (displacement, velocity, acceleration) — direction matters.
  • Pick the kinematic equation that's missing the variable you don't need.
  • On a v–t graph: slope = acceleration, area = displacement.
  • Relative motion: velocities add across reference frames, with signs tracked carefully.
  • 2D motion = two independent 1D problems linked only by time. Projectiles have zero horizontal acceleration and constant vertical acceleration g.
